# Design History: Movements, Context and Transferable Principles

How to use this file: each entry gives period, context, philosophy, characteristics, materials and techniques, representative practitioners and works, strengths, limitations, where it remains useful, and how to apply it without dated imitation. Named works and dates are documented facts unless marked "c." (approximate) or flagged as interpretation. For style-selection guidance see `style-catalogue.md`; for moving principles between disciplines see `cross-disciplinary-design.md`.

---

## 1. Ancient and traditional foundations

These traditions are complete intellectual and technical systems, not decorative source material. Each developed its own theory of proportion, construction and meaning, usually tied to climate, cosmology and available materials.

### Ancient Egypt (c. 3000-30 BCE, Nile valley)
Context: a centralised state, monumental religion, abundant stone, a hot dry climate that preserved work. Philosophy: permanence, order and continuity; art served ritual function, not individual expression. Characteristics: strict proportional canons applied through grid systems on walls and sculpture; axial temple plans; hierarchical scale (size encodes importance); flat registers combining profile and frontal views. Materials and techniques: limestone, granite, faience, gold; copper tools and abrasives; mass labour organisation. Representative works: pyramids at Giza (c. 2560 BCE), temple complexes at Karnak and Luxor. Strengths: unmatched consistency sustained over millennia through codified systems. Weaknesses: rigidity; individual innovation suppressed. Still useful: grid-governed figure construction, hierarchy by scale, designing for permanence. Apply without imitation: borrow the discipline of a proportional canon and hierarchical scale, not lotus columns and hieroglyph ornament.

### Classical Greece (c. 600-146 BCE, Aegean)
Context: city-states, humanist philosophy, mathematics, competitive civic patronage. Philosophy: beauty as measurable proportion; the human body as the reference of measure. Characteristics: the Doric, Ionic and Corinthian orders; modular proportion derived from the column diameter; optical corrections (entasis, the slight swelling of columns, and curvature of the stylobate) to counter visual distortion. Materials: marble, originally painted (the white ideal is a later misreading, a documented fact often forgotten). Representative work: the Parthenon, Athens (c. 447-432 BCE, Iktinos and Kallikrates, sculptural programme associated with Phidias). Strengths: optical correction, the insight that geometric truth and perceived truth differ; a modular system that scales. Weaknesses: a narrow canon of acceptable forms. Still useful: optical correction is the direct ancestor of optical alignment in type and icon design. Apply without imitation: correct for perception, do not add columns.

### Rome (c. 500 BCE-476 CE)
Context: empire, engineering, standardisation, concrete. Philosophy: Vitruvius (1st century BCE) codified firmitas, utilitas, venustas: strength, utility, beauty, the oldest surviving statement that good design balances all three. Characteristics: arches, vaults, domes; infrastructure as design (roads, aqueducts); interior space as the subject. Representative works: the Pantheon (c. 126 CE, the coffered concrete dome with central oculus), Pont du Gard. Strengths: scale, repeatable engineering, logistics. Weaknesses: heavy borrowing of Greek surface language over its own structural innovations. Still useful: the firmitas-utilitas-venustas triad remains a valid review rubric; coffering shows how structure can also be the ornament. Apply without imitation: let structure generate the visual pattern.

### India (c. 2500 BCE onward)
Context: successive traditions, Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, later Indo-Islamic, each with codified treatises. Philosophy: architecture as cosmology; Vastu Shastra and Shilpa Shastra texts prescribe proportion, orientation and iconometry. Characteristics: mandala-based plans; profuse but systematised sculpture integrated with structure; stepwells responding to climate; the stupa as a geometric diagram of the cosmos. Representative works: Sanchi stupa (begun 3rd century BCE), Kailasa temple at Ellora (8th century CE, carved top-down from living rock), the Taj Mahal (1632-1653, Indo-Islamic synthesis, bilateral symmetry, charbagh garden). Strengths: total integration of symbolism, structure and craft; climate-driven typologies. Weaknesses: canonical prescription could override structural experiment. Still useful: modular sacred-geometry planning, density handled through rhythm rather than chaos. Apply without imitation: borrow systematised density and climatic response, not applied temple motifs.

### China (c. 1500 BCE onward)
Context: centralised bureaucracy, timber abundance, codified building standards. Philosophy: harmony, hierarchy and modularity; the Yingzao Fashi (building standards manual, 1103 CE) is a documented early example of a published design system with standard components. Characteristics: timber frames with dougong bracket clusters; standard bay modules; courtyard planning; porcelain and lacquer craft with tight process control. Representative works: Forbidden City (15th century), Song dynasty ceramics. Strengths: the module as the unit of both construction and aesthetics; repairability (joined timber, no nails in principal joints). Weaknesses: codification again limited structural novelty. Still useful: the clearest historical precedent for component libraries and design tokens. Apply without imitation: adopt modular standardisation with expressive assembly, not dragon ornament.

### Japan (c. 600 CE onward)
Context: imported Chinese systems refined under scarcity, earthquakes and Zen Buddhism. Philosophy: ma (meaningful interval), wabi-sabi (beauty in imperfection and impermanence), shakkei (borrowed scenery). Characteristics: the tatami mat as a human-scaled planning module; post-and-beam frames with movable screens; restraint, asymmetry, natural materials left honest. Representative works: Ise shrine (ritually rebuilt on cycle, documented tradition), Katsura Imperial Villa (17th century), Ryōan-ji rock garden. Strengths: whitespace as an active element centuries before modernism named it; module plus asymmetry, order without monotony. Weaknesses: extreme restraint can read as austerity outside its cultural frame. Still useful: directly ancestral to modern minimalism; see Japanese Minimalism in `styles/foundation.md`. Apply without imitation: use interval, asymmetry and material honesty, not shoji-screen graphics.

### Persia (c. 550 BCE onward)
Context: empires at a crossroads of trade; later a centre of Islamic art. Philosophy: the garden as paradise (the chahar bagh four-part garden plan is the root of the word paradise, documented etymology); colour and light as spiritual material. Characteristics: monumental axial planning (Persepolis, begun c. 518 BCE), later turquoise and cobalt tilework, iwans, muqarnas vaulting; carpet design as a portable, codified compositional system. Strengths: colour systems of extraordinary sophistication; the carpet as a masterclass in border, field and medallion composition. Still useful: field-and-border composition maps directly onto page and card layout. Apply without imitation: borrow the compositional grammar and saturated-on-neutral colour logic, not literal arabesques.

### Islamic traditions (7th century CE onward, Spain to South Asia)
Context: aniconism in religious contexts channelled invention into geometry, calligraphy and vegetal pattern. Philosophy: infinite pattern as an expression of the infinite; mathematics as a design medium. Characteristics: girih strapwork, star-and-polygon tessellations, muqarnas, calligraphy as the highest art form. Representative works: the Alhambra, Granada (mainly 14th century), the Great Mosque of Córdoba, Mughal architecture. Strengths: generative, rule-based pattern centuries before computational design; seamless scaling of one geometric idea from tile to building. Weaknesses: none inherent; the common failure is modern misuse as shallow texture. Still useful: the direct historical ancestor of generative and parametric pattern systems. Apply without imitation: derive patterns from an explicit geometric rule, do not paste "oriental" texture.

## 2. Renaissance through the eighteenth century

### Renaissance (c. 1400-1600, Italy then Europe)
Context: humanism, rediscovered classical texts, merchant patronage, printing. Philosophy: man as the measure; art as a rational, learnable discipline. Key developments: Brunelleschi's dome for Florence cathedral (completed 1436) and his demonstration of linear perspective; Alberti's De pictura (1435) codifying perspective; Leonardo da Vinci's unification of observation, anatomy and art; Palladio's Four Books of Architecture (1570), the most influential pattern book in Western architecture. Strengths: perspective gave design a reproducible spatial system; proportion theory connected maths to beauty. Weaknesses: later academies froze its rules into dogma. Still useful: perspective construction, proportional facade logic, the idea that craft can be codified and taught. Apply without imitation: use constructed space and proportional systems, not putti and pilasters.

### Baroque (c. 1600-1750)
Context: Counter-Reformation persuasion, absolutist courts. Philosophy: move the viewer; drama as rhetoric. Characteristics: diagonal composition, deep chiaroscuro (Caravaggio's tenebrism), curved plans, controlled theatrical light (Bernini's Ecstasy of Saint Teresa, 1647-1652). Strengths: mastery of directed attention, light as a compositional tool. Weaknesses: expense; emotional manipulation as a stated goal. Still useful: cinematic lighting, focal control, staging a reveal; see Cinematic in `styles/typographic-editorial.md`. Apply without imitation: borrow the light choreography, not gilt and drapery.

### Rococo (c. 1720-1770, France outward)
Context: aristocratic interiors, intimacy after Versailles grandeur. Characteristics: asymmetric rocaille ornament, pastels, lightness, wit. Strengths: proof that playfulness and fine craft coexist. Weaknesses: ornament unmoored from structure; swept away by Neoclassical reaction. Still useful: a reference for decorative exuberance executed with control. Apply without imitation: take the lightness and asymmetric flow, not scrollwork.

### Neoclassicism and the eighteenth century (c. 1750-1820)
Context: excavation of Pompeii and Herculaneum (from 1748), the Enlightenment, early industrial production. Characteristics: restraint, archaeological correctness, clear geometry; in furniture, documented workshops and pattern books (Thomas Chippendale's Director, 1754). Scientific context: rapid advances in optics and descriptive geometry fed exact technical representation. Strengths: discipline and clarity recovered after Rococo. Weaknesses: moralising severity; copying antiquity rather than reasoning from it. Still useful: restraint as a deliberate statement; the pattern book as a product-family system. Apply without imitation: adopt the restraint and geometric clarity, not urns and swags.

## 3. Nineteenth century

The century's core tension, documented in its own debates: handcraft versus mass production.

### Victorian design (c. 1837-1901, Britain and empire)
Context: industrialisation, new money, historicist eclecticism; the Great Exhibition of 1851 in Paxton's Crystal Palace (itself a radical prefabricated iron-and-glass building housing ornament-heavy exhibits, an irony noted by contemporaries). Characteristics: dense ornament, mixed revival styles, horror vacui interiors, elaborate display typography (fat faces, Tuscans, chromolithography). Strengths: fearless typographic invention; engineering beneath the ornament (bridges, stations). Weaknesses: ornament applied without system; the mismatch critics like Ruskin attacked. Still useful: layered display typography for heritage and maximalist work; see Victorian in `styles/heritage.md`. Apply without imitation: borrow layered type hierarchy and density with a governing system, not random flourish.

### Gothic Revival (c. 1830-1900)
Context: A. W. N. Pugin and John Ruskin argued Gothic was honest construction with moral weight; Pugin co-designed the Palace of Westminster interiors (rebuilt from 1840). Philosophy: truth to structure and material; ornament should decorate construction, never conceal it. Strengths: the first modern articulation of material honesty, later a modernist axiom. Weaknesses: religious moralising; expensive craft. Still useful: "decorate construction, do not construct decoration" remains one of the sharpest design tests ever written (Pugin's principle, paraphrased). 

### Arts and Crafts (c. 1860-1910, Britain then worldwide)
Context: William Morris's reaction to industrial shoddiness; Morris & Co. founded 1861; Red House (Philip Webb for Morris, 1859). Philosophy: joy in labour, unity of designer and maker, honest materials, art for everyday life. Characteristics: flat pattern from nature, visible joinery, vernacular forms. Strengths: elevated everyday objects; founded design reform education. Weaknesses: the documented contradiction Morris himself acknowledged, handcraft priced out the ordinary people it was meant for. Still useful: craft standards, pattern design discipline, the ethics of how things are made. Apply without imitation: adopt the care and material honesty, not willow-pattern wallpaper.

### Art Nouveau (c. 1890-1910, pan-European under many names)
Context: a deliberate attempt at a new style owing nothing to revivalism; new materials (iron, glass) and lithographic printing. Characteristics: whiplash line, organic asymmetry, integration of structure and ornament. Practitioners: Victor Horta (Hôtel Tassel, 1893), Hector Guimard (Paris Métro entrances, c. 1900), Alphonse Mucha (poster work), Antoni Gaudí (a related but distinct Catalan strand), Charles Rennie Mackintosh (the more rectilinear Glasgow school). Strengths: total design, from building to doorknob; organic line with structural logic. Weaknesses: costly craft; stylistic exhaustion within two decades. Still useful: organic line systems, letterform-and-image integration; see Art Nouveau in `styles/heritage.md`. Apply without imitation: derive curves from growth logic and keep structural continuity, do not trace Mucha borders.

### Industrialisation itself as a design force
Michael Thonet's bentwood chair No. 14 (1859) is the canonical case: steam-bent beech, few parts, shipped flat in volume. Interpretation widely accepted by historians: it anticipates flat-pack logistics and demonstrates that process constraints can generate iconic form. The century's lesson: the craft-versus-industry tension was never resolved, it was synthesised piecemeal, and every later movement takes a position on it.

## 4. Twentieth century

### De Stijl (1917-c. 1931, Netherlands)
Founded around the journal De Stijl (Theo van Doesburg, 1917). Philosophy: universal harmony through pure abstraction; only primaries plus black, white, grey; only horizontals and verticals. Works: Mondrian's compositions; Rietveld's Red and Blue Chair (c. 1918-1923) and Schröder House (1924). Strengths: radical reduction proved composition alone can carry a work. Weaknesses: dogma (van Doesburg's diagonal caused a documented split with Mondrian). Still useful: asymmetric balance, planes of colour as structure. Apply without imitation: use the balancing method, not the red-yellow-blue cliché.

### Constructivism (c. 1915-early 1930s, Russia)
Context: revolution; art as production for society. Characteristics: diagonals, photomontage, bold sans type, red-black palettes. Practitioners: Alexander Rodchenko, El Lissitzky (Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge, 1919), Gustav Klutsis; Tatlin's Monument to the Third International (1919-1920, unbuilt). Strengths: graphic urgency; the poster as weapon; early total integration of photography and type. Weaknesses: tied to a political programme that then suppressed it (documented end under Stalinist doctrine). Still useful: dynamic diagonal composition, type as image. Apply without imitation: borrow the energy and montage logic, not Soviet iconography.

### Bauhaus (1919-1933, Weimar, Dessau, Berlin)
Founded by Walter Gropius; closed under Nazi pressure in 1933 (documented). Philosophy: unite art, craft and industry; form follows function taught through a foundation course (Vorkurs) under Itten, later Moholy-Nagy. Masters and works: Marcel Breuer's tubular steel B3 chair (c. 1925), Marianne Brandt's metalwork, Herbert Bayer's universal typography experiments, Mies van der Rohe (final director). Strengths: the modern design-school model; geometry plus industrial material; workshop teaching. Weaknesses: its universalism ignored culture and climate; some output was craft pretending to be industrial. Still useful: foundational pedagogy, the primary-geometry toolkit, honest industrial materials; see Bauhaus in `styles/foundation.md`. Apply without imitation: reason from function and material, do not stamp circles and triangles in red-yellow-blue.

### Art Deco (c. 1920-1939, Paris outward; named from the 1925 Exposition Internationale des Arts Décoratifs)
Context: machine-age optimism, jazz, luxury trades, then Depression-era streamlining. Characteristics: symmetry, stepped forms, sunbursts, exotic materials (and early plastics); geometric sans and inline display type. Works: Chrysler Building (1930, William Van Alen), Cassandre's posters (Normandie, 1935). Strengths: glamour with geometric discipline; scales from jewellery to skyscraper. Weaknesses: surface-first; ornament often independent of structure. Still useful: luxury branding, hospitality, display type; see Art Deco in `styles/heritage.md`. Apply without imitation: keep the geometric discipline and metallic restraint, drop the pastiche sunburst unless the brief is explicitly period.

### Modernism and the International Style (c. 1920s-1970s)
The architectural mainstream after the 1932 MoMA exhibition named the International Style (Hitchcock and Johnson, documented). Philosophy: function, new materials (steel, glass, concrete), rejection of ornament (Adolf Loos's essay Ornament and Crime, 1908, was the polemic groundwork). Works: Le Corbusier's Villa Savoye (1931) and his Modulor proportional system; Mies's Seagram Building (1958, with Philip Johnson), "less is more" (attributed to Mies). Strengths: spatial clarity, honest structure, universal ambitions produced genuinely new building types. Weaknesses: documented failures when universalism met real climates and communities (public housing critiques); hostility to ornament became its own dogma. Still useful: open plans, curtain-wall logic, proportional systems. Apply without imitation: adopt clarity and structural honesty; do not treat whiteness and flat roofs as mandatory.

### Swiss / International Typographic Style (c. 1950s-1970s, Basel and Zurich)
Context: postwar neutrality, pharmaceutical and industrial clients needing multilingual clarity. Philosophy: objective communication; the grid as an ethical stance. Characteristics: mathematical grids, asymmetric layouts, sans-serif type (Helvetica, 1957, Max Miedinger with Eduard Hoffmann; Univers, 1957, Adrian Frutiger), photography over illustration, flush-left ragged-right. Practitioners: Josef Müller-Brockmann (Grid Systems in Graphic Design, 1981), Armin Hofmann, Emil Ruder. Strengths: the most transferable layout system ever devised; the direct ancestor of responsive grids and design systems. Weaknesses: can be cold; "objectivity" is itself a style, an interpretation its own practitioners later conceded. Still useful: everywhere; see Swiss in `styles/foundation.md`. Apply without imitation: build the grid from content and format, do not just set Helvetica and call it Swiss.

### Mid-century modern (c. 1945-1969, USA and Europe)
Context: postwar prosperity, new materials from war industry (plywood moulding, fibreglass), suburban optimism. Works: Charles and Ray Eames (moulded plywood and fibreglass chairs, Eames House 1949), George Nelson, Eero Saarinen (Tulip chair, TWA terminal 1962). Strengths: warmth plus industry, the proof that modernism could be domestic and humane. Weaknesses: now the most imitated and diluted of all styles. Still useful: organic curves with industrial process; see Mid-Century Modern in `styles/heritage.md`.

### Scandinavian design (c. 1930s onward)
Context: social democracy, long winters, timber economies. Philosophy: beautiful everyday goods for everyone (documented regional ideal, e.g. Swedish "vackrare vardagsvara"). Practitioners: Alvar Aalto (bent plywood, Paimio chair c. 1932), Arne Jacobsen, Hans Wegner, Poul Henningsen (PH lamps engineered for glare-free light). Strengths: humanised modernism, light as a designed material, democratic pricing ambitions (later IKEA's industrialisation of the ideal). Weaknesses: gentle sameness when applied without conviction. Still useful: warm minimalism, honest wood, lighting design; see `styles/foundation.md`.

### Japanese modernism (c. 1950s onward)
Context: postwar reconstruction; synthesis of craft tradition and industrial method. Practitioners and works: Sori Yanagi (Butterfly stool, 1954), Kenzo Tange (Hiroshima Peace Centre, Yoyogi gymnasia 1964), later Kenya Hara's art direction for MUJI (from 2002) and Naoto Fukasawa's "without thought" designs. Strengths: emptiness as capacity (Hara's stated concept), tolerance-level craft in industrial goods. Still useful: the strongest living bridge between ancient restraint and contemporary product design.

### Pop Art (c. 1955-1970, Britain and USA)
Richard Hamilton, Andy Warhol (Campbell's Soup Cans, 1962), Roy Lichtenstein (Ben-Day dot paintings). Philosophy: mass culture as subject matter; repetition and commercial print as technique. Strengths: colour at full volume, screen-print flatness, irony. Weaknesses: irony curdles fast in commercial reuse. Still useful: bold flat colour, repetition grids, halftone texture; see `styles/illustration-expressive.md`.

### Brutalism (c. 1950s-1970s architecture; separate later web usage)
From béton brut, raw concrete (term popularised via Le Corbusier's Unité d'Habitation, 1952, and the Smithsons in Britain, documented). Philosophy: material and structure exposed without apology; often socially idealistic public buildings (Barbican Estate, Boston City Hall). Strengths: massing, honesty, sculptural shadow. Weaknesses: maintenance in wet climates; became shorthand for institutional failure. Web brutalism and neo-brutalism are distinct descendants, see `styles/foundation.md` and `styles/contemporary-specialist.md`.

### Memphis (1981-1987, Milan)
Founded by Ettore Sottsass with young designers (first collection 1981; Carlton bookcase). Philosophy: against good taste; colour, pattern (laminates), toy-like geometry, deliberate clashing. Strengths: permission to be loud; proof that furniture can argue. Weaknesses: much of it barely functional, by intent. Still useful: controlled clash, pattern-on-pattern; its 2010s graphic revival shows both the power and the trap of revival styles.

### Postmodernism (c. 1966-1990s)
Robert Venturi's Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture (1966) and Learning from Las Vegas (1972, with Scott Brown and Izenour) are the founding texts; "less is a bore" (Venturi). Characteristics: historical quotation, irony, colour, the decorated shed. Works: Michael Graves's Portland Building (1982), AT&T Building (Johnson and Burgee, 1984). Strengths: restored meaning, wit and context to the conversation. Weaknesses: quotation decayed into developer kitsch. Still useful: the critique stands, pure functionalism underserves human meaning.

### Minimalism (1960s art onward; design usage broader)
Donald Judd, Agnes Martin, Dan Flavin in art; in design, the lineage runs through Braun and Dieter Rams (chief designer from 1961; his "ten principles of good design" and "less, but better" are documented) to John Pawson in architecture and Jony Ive's acknowledged debt to Rams at Apple. Strengths: clarity, longevity, respect for attention. Weaknesses: emptiness mistaken for rigour; minimal surfaces can hide complex, unaccountable behaviour. See Minimalism in `styles/foundation.md`.

### Industrial design as a profession
Streamlining era: Raymond Loewy, Henry Dreyfuss (whose Designing for People, 1955, and human-measure charts founded ergonomic practice), Norman Bel Geddes. Ulm School of Design (1953-1968) formalised systematic method and fed Braun. Lesson: the discipline professionalised twice, once through styling (Loewy) and once through method (Ulm/Dreyfuss); both lineages matter. See `industrial-design.md`.

### Early digital aesthetics (c. 1970s-1990s)
Xerox PARC's GUI research (1970s), the Apple Macintosh (1984) with Susan Kare's bitmap icons and fonts, Emigre magazine and early digital type (Zuzana Licko, from 1984), April Greiman's digital collage. Characteristics: pixel grids as a creative constraint, experimentation licensed by new tools. Still useful: pixel-grid discipline survives in icon design and Pixel Art; the era proves constraints breed styles.

## 5. Late twentieth century to the present

### Web and interface evolution (c. 1991 onward)
Documented arc: text-only pages (1991), table-based layout and GIFs (mid-90s), Flash experimentation (late 90s-2000s), the web-standards movement and CSS layout (early 2000s), skeuomorphic richness peaking in early iOS (2007-2012), then the flat correction: Microsoft's Metro language (c. 2010), iOS 7 (2013), Google's Material Design (2014, flat plus physically-modelled depth and motion). Responsive web design named by Ethan Marcotte (2010). Lesson: interface fashion oscillates between depth and flatness; each swing overcorrects, then recovers affordances (flat design's usability losses are documented in usability research, e.g. Nielsen Norman Group critiques).

### Design systems era (c. 2014 onward)
Atomic Design (Brad Frost, book 2016), token-based theming, Figma's multiplayer model (general release 2016). Shift of the unit of design from the page to the component. Lesson: this is Yingzao Fashi and the pattern book again, standard parts plus rules; history repeats with better tooling.

### Skeuomorphism, glassmorphism, Liquid Glass
Skeuomorphism (material metaphors for learnability) never died, it retreated to moments where affordance needs help. Glassmorphism (translucent blur layers) entered mainstream OS design via Windows Vista Aero (2006), iOS 7 blurs, Windows 11 Mica; Apple's Liquid Glass design language (announced WWDC, June 2025) is the current high-polish form, with documented legibility criticisms during its beta cycle. Treat all of these as depth-management tools with known contrast risks; see `styles/digital-dimensional.md`.

### Generative and computational design (c. 1960s research, mainstream 2010s onward)
Lineage: Vera Molnár and early plotter art (1960s), Processing (Reas and Fry, 2001), parametric architecture (Zaha Hadid Architects, Grasshopper tooling), data-driven identity systems (e.g. MIT Media Lab's 2011 generative identity, documented). Principle: design the rule, not the artefact. The Islamic girih tradition is the deep precedent.

### Contemporary editorial, cyberpunk and retro-futurism
Editorial design migrated web-ward (large type, strong grids, art-directed scroll). Cyberpunk's visual canon is documented fiction sources (Blade Runner, 1982; Neuromancer, 1984; Ghost in the Shell, 1995): neon-on-dark density, urban layering. Retro-futurism mines past futures (Space Age, Atomic Age). All three are mood languages: see `styles/contemporary-specialist.md` for use-with-care notes.

### Sustainable and circular design (gathering force 1990s onward)
From Victor Papanek's Design for the Real World (1971) through cradle-to-cradle (McDonough and Braungart, 2002) to current right-to-repair legislation (EU directives in force, verify current state when relevant). Principle set: longevity, repairability, material cycles, honest footprint claims. This is a constraint system, not a style, though an aesthetic of visible repair and mono-materials has grown around it.

### AI-assisted and generative design (c. 2022 onward, fast-moving)
Diffusion-model imagery, LLM-assisted layout and code, prompt-based art direction. Durable principles: the brief and the judgement remain human work; verify factual and brand claims in generated output; distinguish a generated mock-up from a built, accessible artefact. Specific tool capabilities change too fast to record here, verify at time of use (noted 2026).

## 6. Future-facing design (emerging, not established fact)

These are active research and market directions, recorded 2026, to be re-verified before relying on them: adaptive and context-aware interfaces; spatial computing (headset platforms exist, interaction conventions unsettled); generative product forms with additive manufacturing; new sustainable materials (mycelium composites, low-carbon concrete, in varying maturity); autonomous-system interfaces (trust calibration and handover are the live human-factors problems); human-centred AI experience patterns (disclosure, controllability, graceful failure). Treat every claim in this section as provisional and date any use of it.

## 7. Threads across history (the transferable ideas)

1. **Craft versus mass production.** Morris versus Thonet, Ulm versus Loewy, artisanal versus platform today. Transfer: decide where handwork earns its cost and where systematisation serves people better; the best work (Eames, Aalto, Rams) synthesises both.
2. **Ornament versus restraint.** Rococo to Neoclassicism, Victorian to Arts and Crafts, Loos to Venturi, skeuomorphic to flat. The pendulum is the lesson: every purge overcorrects and loses affordance or meaning, every indulgence loses clarity. Transfer: hold position by purpose, not by fashion phase.
3. **Grid and module thinking.** Egyptian canons, tatami, Yingzao Fashi, Palladio, Swiss grids, design tokens. Transfer: a module system is the oldest proven route to coherence at scale; the craft is choosing the module from content and human measure.
4. **Material honesty.** Pugin, Ruskin, Bauhaus, Brutalism, flat design (pixels honest about being pixels, an interpretation but a useful one). Transfer: let the medium behave as itself; faked materials are a debt the design repays in trust.
5. **Optical truth over geometric truth.** Greek entasis, Renaissance perspective, typographic optical alignment, icon grids. Transfer: always correct for perception; measure with eyes last.
6. **Structure as ornament.** Pantheon coffers, Gothic ribs, dougong brackets, exposed grids in Swiss layout. Transfer: before adding decoration, ask what the existing structure could express if revealed.
7. **Rule-based generation.** Girih, Persian carpets, serial music-adjacent art, parametric and AI systems. Transfer: when variety at scale is needed, design the rule and its constraints, then curate outputs.
8. **Design as social claim.** Arts and Crafts ethics, Constructivist production art, Scandinavian democracy, sustainability. Transfer: every pricing, durability and accessibility decision is a statement about who the work is for; make it deliberately.
9. **Codification versus invention.** Treatises, pattern books and design systems lock in quality and risk locking out progress. Transfer: version the system, schedule its revision, and record why each rule exists so it can be safely broken.
10. **Light as material.** Baroque theatre, Henningsen lamps, architectural daylighting, interface elevation and blur. Transfer: treat light and depth as designed, budgeted elements with hierarchy jobs, not after-effects.
