# Architecture and Spatial Design

Architecture organises space, structure, light and movement for human use over decades. It is the slowest and most consequential design discipline: its mistakes are inhabited. Its principles, however, travel far beyond buildings.

## Massing and proportion

- Massing is the first decision: the overall volumes and their relationships read before any detail. A building with wrong massing cannot be rescued by facade treatment.
- Proportion systems (classical orders, modular grids, simple ratios) are tools for relating parts to wholes. The value is the relationship, not any single magic number.
- Hierarchy of volumes: primary mass, secondary masses, connective tissue. The eye should understand the organisation from across the street.

## Circulation

- Circulation is the user journey of a building: entry, orientation, movement, pause, exit.
- Arrival sequence matters: compression and release (a low entry opening into a tall hall) is an emotional instrument.
- People should never need signage to find the obvious: the architecture itself should suggest where to go. Wayfinding graphics compensate for, they do not replace, legible circulation.
- Service circulation and public circulation are separated so each can be good at its job.

## Light and shadow

- Light is a material. Orientation, aperture size, depth of reveal and surface finish decide the quality of light, not the lamp catalogue.
- Direct light dramatises; diffuse light calms; raking light reveals texture. Choose per room purpose.
- Shadow gives form its depth. A facade without shadow play is a drawing, not a building.
- Daylight varies by hour and season: a space is designed for its worst light, not its photo light.

## Material transitions

- Where two materials meet is where craft is judged: the junction is either resolved (reveal, shadow gap, trim, flush detail) or it is a visible apology.
- Materials change at logical places: a change in plane, function or volume, not mid-surface.
- Heavy below, light above reads as stable; inverting it is a deliberate statement with structural implications.

## Structure and function

- Structure can be expressed or concealed; both are valid, but the choice must be consistent and honest. Decorative fake structure corrodes the whole design.
- The plan is the generator: spaces sized and placed for their use, structure serving that plan, facade reporting both.
- Flexibility has a cost: a space for everything serves nothing perfectly. Decide which functions are fixed and which must adapt.

## Human scale

- Buildings are measured by bodies: riser heights, handrails, sill heights, ceiling heights, door weights. Scale errors are felt before they are seen.
- Big buildings need scale steps: elements sized to the city, to the street, to the person, layered so humans are never confronted by a single inhuman surface.

## Acoustics and environment

- Every material choice is also an acoustic choice: hard surfaces reflect, soft absorb, volume lengthens reverberation.
- Rooms for speech, music, work and rest have different acoustic targets; ignoring them makes a beautiful room unusable.

## Climate responsiveness

- Orientation, shading, thermal mass, ventilation and envelope quality come before mechanical systems. The best climate machine is the building's shape.
- Regional tradition encodes climate intelligence: deep verandas, courtyards, thick walls, wind catchers evolved for a reason. Learn the principle before importing the look.

## Construction constraints

- Everything must be buildable in sequence by real trades with real tolerances; a detail that cannot be assembled is not a detail.
- Buildings move: expansion, settlement, moisture. Joints and tolerances are designed, not hoped away.
- Budget is a material: knowing where to spend (the places hands and eyes touch) and where to economise is a core design skill.

## Interior and exterior

- The threshold is a designed experience, not a wall with a hole.
- Views are composed: what you see from inside, and what the building shows the street, are both facades.
- Interior logic should be legible outside; a building that lies about its interior feels wrong even to people who cannot say why.

## Evaluation checklist (spatial or layout work)

1. Thumbnail test: do the big masses read correctly at small size, before any detail?
2. Entry: is there one obvious starting point, and does the sequence from it make sense?
3. Rhythm: is there an underlying module, and are breaks from it deliberate?
4. Junctions: is every meeting of materials, sections or components resolved, not accidental?
5. Scale steps: are there distinct levels of scale (building/street/person, or page/section/line)?
6. Light and value: is there one brightest moment, with everything else supporting it?
7. Negative space: are the empty areas shaped and intentional, or leftover?
8. Context: does the design respond to its actual site or medium, or is it placeless?

## Transferable principles

These spatial principles transfer directly to layouts, posters, web pages, slides and brand systems:

- **Structural grid**: a consistent underlying grid lets varied content feel ordered; columns and modules in a layout are bays and bays are rhythm.
- **Massing first**: block out the big shapes of a page (hero, body, aside) before any detail, and judge the composition at thumbnail size.
- **Circulation**: the reader's path through a page is designed like movement through a building: entry point, sequence, pauses, exit. Compression and release become dense and open sections.
- **Negative space as a room**: whitespace is not absence; it is where the eye rests, exactly as a courtyard is not wasted floor area.
- **Human scale**: type sizes, touch targets and line lengths are the sill heights of a layout; scale steps (display, heading, body, caption) keep large compositions humane.
- **Material transitions**: component boundaries, section breaks and background changes are junctions; resolve them deliberately (spacing, rule, shift in value) rather than letting surfaces collide.
- **Light discipline**: in visual work, treat contrast and value structure as architects treat daylight: decide where the brightest moment is, and let everything else support it.
- **Climate responsiveness**: adapt the same design to its context (screen size, print stock, viewing distance) the way a building adapts to its site.

## Domain-specific constraints that do NOT transfer

- Structural feasibility, fire safety, egress, accessibility codes and energy compliance are regulated engineering domains. Never casually claim a concept "is structurally sound", "meets code" or "is buildable"; present concepts as concepts and name the validation they still need.
- Real architecture is bound to site, climate, soil and jurisdiction; a spatial idea without a site is a diagram, and should be labelled as one.
- Cost and programme claims (build cost, timeline) require quantity surveying, not styling judgement.
